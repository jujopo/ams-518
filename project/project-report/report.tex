	\begin{abstract}
		This project analyzes the performance of a diversified equity portfolio through two distinct investor personas—a Buy \& Hold investor and a Hedge Fund–style daily mark-to-market investor—using a decade of historical market data. The study evaluates risk-adjusted returns, drawdowns, and distributional properties of P\&L streams under multiple weighting methodologies, including equal weighting, market capitalization weighting, geometrically weighting, and logarithmic market capitalization weighting. In addition, the project develops an index-tracking model designed to replicate the performance of the 10-stock target portfolio using a subset of S\&P 500 constituents, constrained by cardinality and minimum position size requirements.
		
		Data were collected using the \texttt{yfinance} API and processed to generate return series, volatility estimates, and risk metrics such as Sharpe Ratio, Value at Risk (VaR), and Conditional VaR (CVaR). The results highlight the trade-offs between concentration risk, diversification, portfolio turnover, and tracking accuracy. The findings illustrate how portfolio construction techniques and investor objectives critically shape realized performance and downside risk.
	\end{abstract}
	
	
	\section{Introduction}
	
	
	Modern portfolio theory (MPT), or mean-variance analysis, is a mathematical framework for assembling a portfolio of assets such that the expected return is maximized for a given level of risk. It is a formalization and extension of diversification in investing, the idea that owning different kinds of financial assets is less risky than owning only one type. Its key insight is that an asset's risk and return should not be assessed by itself, but by how it contributes to a portfolio's overall risk and return. The variance of return (or its transformation, the standard deviation) is used as a measure of risk, because it is tractable when assets are combined into portfolios.

	\subsection{Core problem definition}

	The core analysis is centered on a portfolio composed of 10 U.S. stocks, which were selected to represent a mix of technology, dividend aristocrat\footnote{A dividend aristocrat is an S\&P 500 company that has increased its dividend annually for at least 25 years, demonstrating a commitment to returning cash to investors}, and high-dividend yield sectors. The portfolio is defined by
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Technology:} MSFT, AAPL, IBM
		\item \textbf{Dividend aristocrat:} XOM, WTM, KO, JNJ
		\item \textbf{High dividend yield:} VZ, KHC, USB
	\end{itemize}
	The performance of this 10-stock target portfolio will be evaluated using historical market data, we assume some specific weighting strategies defined in Section \ref{Weighting}. 
	
	\subsection{Persona models (Stochastic Models)}
	The performance of the target portfolio is evaluated through two personas, each representing a different investment horizon and objective. The P\&L for each is modeled as a stochastic process dependent on the historical price path of the portfolio's assets.
	
	\subsubsection{Buy \& Hold (BH) model}
	This persona represents a long-term investor who acquires the portfolio at the beginning of the analysis period and holds it until the end.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Objective:} Maximize total return over the entire period, inclusive of all cash dividends. 
		\item \textbf{Model:} The P\&L is a single-stage calculation: $\text{P\&L}_\text{BH} = \text{Value}_{E} - \text{Value}_{S}$
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsubsection{Hedge Fund (HF) model}
	This persona represents an institutional investor that marks-to-market\footnote{Accounting method that values assets and liabilities at their current market price rather than their historical or original cost} and captures P\&L changes on a daily basis.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Objective:} Generate consistent, positive daily returns (\textit{alpha}) while managing daily volatility.
		\item \textbf{Model:} The PnL is a multi-stage stochastic series: $\text{P\&L}_{HF}(t) = \text{Value}_t - \text{Value}_{t-1}$ for each day $t$.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Risk Constraints and Performance Metrics}
	A simple return analysis is insufficient. We introduce risk constraints by evaluating each persona's P\&L stream using standard performance metrics.
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Sharpe Ratio:} Measures risk-adjusted return, defined as:
		\begin{equation}
			\text{Sharpe Ratio} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[R_p - R_f]}{\sigma[R_p - R_f]}
		\end{equation}
		where $R_p$ is the portfolio's return, $R_f$ is the risk-free rate, and $\sigma_p := \sigma[R_p - R_f]$ is the standard deviation (volatility) of the portfolio's excess return.
		
		\item \textbf{Value at Risk (VaR):} Defines the maximum expected loss at a given confidence level $1-\alpha$. It is the $1-\alpha$ quantile of the P\&L distribution, $F_X(x)$.
		\begin{equation}
			\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(X) = - \inf \{x \in \mathbb{R} : F_X(x) > \alpha\}
		\end{equation}
		For this project, we will use the non-parametric Historical Simulation method.
		
		\item \textbf{Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR):} Measures the expected loss \textit{given} that the loss exceeds the VaR.
		\begin{equation}
			\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X) = \mathbb{E}[-X \mid -X \ge \text{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)]
		\end{equation}
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{Index Tracking Model (Optimization Model)}
	This task involves creating a replicating portfolio from a universe of other stocks (e.g., S\&P 500 components excluding the 10 target stocks) to mimic the returns of our target portfolio. This is formulated as a constrained optimization problem.
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Objective Function:} Minimize the Tracking Error Variance (TEV), which is the variance of the difference between the target portfolio's returns ($R_T$) and the replicating portfolio's returns ($R_R$).
		\begin{equation}
			\min_{w} \quad \text{TEV} = E\left[ \left( R_T(t) - R_R(t) \right)^2 \right]
		\end{equation}
		where $R_R(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i R_i(t)$ and $w_i$ is the weight of stock $i$ in the replicating portfolio.
		
		\item \textbf{Constraints:} The optimization is subject to several constraints:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Full Investment:} Weights must sum to one.
			\begin{equation}
				\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i = 1
			\end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Long-Only:} No short selling is permitted.
			\begin{equation}
				w_i \ge 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\}
			\end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Cardinality Constraint:} The number of stocks in the replicating portfolio must not exceed $k$ (e.g., $k=20$). This is represented using the $L_0$-norm:
			\begin{equation}
				\|w\|_0 \le k
			\end{equation}
			\item \textbf{Buy-In Constraint:} Any selected stock must have a minimum weight of $l$ (e.g., $l=1\%$). This makes the weights semi-continuous:
			\begin{equation}
				w_i \in \{0\} \cup [l, 1] \quad \forall i
			\end{equation}
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Data Acquisition and Preparation}
	
	The first stage of the project consisted of collecting historical price data for the selected U.S. equities over a multi-year horizon.  
	Following the project requirements, we used a data window ranging from one to ten years, depending on the specific analysis.  
	Historical prices, dividends, and corporate‐action–adjusted series were obtained using the \texttt{yfinance} Python package, which provides a simple interface to query the Yahoo Finance API.
	
	\paragraph{Data Download.}
	A dedicated Python script, \texttt{fetch\_prices.py}, was developed to automate the extraction of price data across multiple tickers.  
	The script supports lists of tickers, allowing batch downloads across arbitrary time horizons and sampling intervals.
	\begin{verbatim}
		python fetch_prices.py --tickers AAPL MSFT TSLA --period 5y --interval 1d
	\end{verbatim}
	\vspace{-6mm}
	Data were downloaded using the parameters:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Period:} from 1 year to 10 years of lookback;
		\item \textbf{Interval:} daily data (\texttt{1d}), with optional support for intraday data (\texttt{1m}, \texttt{5m}, \texttt{1h}) for day-trading and high–frequency analysis;
		\item \textbf{Price adjustment:} the \texttt{auto\_adjust=True} option was used to obtain series corrected for dividends, stock splits, and other corporate actions.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The script stores all downloaded data in a structured directory system under \texttt{data/raw/}, ensuring reproducibility and transparency.  
	To maintain consistency across analyses, the script also standardizes filenames according to ticker, period, and interval.
	
	
	\paragraph{Limitations of Intraday Data Availability.}
	During the collection of the data, we faced an important limitation related to the use of \texttt{yfinance} API for intraday data. Yahoo Finance restricts the amount of historical data available at different frequencies, which affects the analysis involving intraday strategies such as the day trader persona. We specifically observed the following constraints:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \texttt{1-minute} data: available only for the last \(\approx 8\) days,
		\item \texttt{5-minute} data: limited to the last \(\approx 60\) days,
		\item \texttt{1-hour} data: available for roughly the last \(\approx 730\) days,
		\item daily data: no meaningful historical limitation.
	\end{itemize}
	
	These restrictions mean that long-horizon intraday performance cannot be analyzed directly using Yahoo Finance. As a result, intraday analysis in this project is necessarily constrained to shorter windows unless alternative data providers (such as Polygon.io, Intrinio, or Tiingo) are used. Daily-frequency data, however, are not subject to these limitations and were therefore used as the primary source for long-term event window, volatility, and persona-level analyses.

	\subsubsection{Equal Weighting (Naïve Diversification)}
	The baseline approach assigns an identical allocation to every asset in the portfolio, regardless of size or risk profile.
	\begin{equation}
		w_i = \frac{1}{N} \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\}
	\end{equation}
	where $N$ is the number of selected equities.  
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Advantages:} Maximizes diversification breadth; mathematically simple; avoids concentration risk and simplifies interpretation of portfolio-level metrics.
		\item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Ignores the economic reality of firm size (treating Kraft Heinz as equal to Apple); requires frequent rebalancing to maintain $1/N$ weights as prices drift.
	\end{itemize}
	
	In Figure \ref{im:equally_weighted} we can see the evolution of wealth over time, we also note that the sharpe ratio obtained is not the most desirable one. Total return and maximum drawdown are also displayed.

	\subsubsection{Standard Market Capitalization Weighting}
	This method allocates capital proportional to the total market value ($M_i$) of each firm, reflecting the standard index construction (e.g., S\&P 500).
	\begin{equation}
		w_i = \frac{M_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} M_j}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Advantages:} Momentum-based (winners get larger weights); theoretically efficient in the CAPM framework; low turnover.
		\item \textbf{Disadvantages:} \textbf{Extreme concentration risk.} In our specific portfolio, the ``Mega-Caps'' (MSFT, AAPL) are two orders of magnitude larger than the smallest firms. This results in the top 2 stocks dictating over 80\% of the portfolio's performance, rendering the diversification into the other 8 assets mathematically negligible. See table \ref{tab:mkt_cap}
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection{Logarithmic Market Capitalization Weighting}
	To address the skewness problem identified in the standard market cap approach, we implemented a smoothing function using natural logarithms. This compresses the scale: logarithms drastically shrink the difference between huge numbers. For example, a \$3 trillion company ($3 \times 10^{12}$) has a log value of $\approx 28.7$. A \$100 billion company ($1 \times 10^{11}$) has a log value of $\approx 25.3$.
	
	The \$3T company is 30$\times$ bigger in real dollars, but only ~1.13$\times$ bigger in ``log-weight'' terms. This preserves the hierarchy (bigger is still heavier) but ensures the smaller stocks get a meaningful allocation.
	
	\begin{equation}
		w_i = \frac{\ln(M_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \ln(M_j)}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Advantages:} Preserves the ordinal rank of size (larger companies still get more weight) but drastically compresses the spread. This allows the ``Mega-Caps'' to lead without dominating, ensuring the dividend aristocrats and high yield stocks have a meaningful impact on total return.
		\item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Heuristic in nature; effectively overweights smaller companies relative to the broad market.
	\end{itemize}
	
	Figure \ref{im:log_mktcap_weighted} represents the evolution of wealth over time for this logarithmic market cap weighted strategy, we can see that the sharpe ratio for this weighting strategy is better than the benchmark. Total return and maximum drawdown are also displayed.

	\subsubsection{Inverse Volatility Weighting (Risk Parity)}
	This risk-aware strategy allocates capital based on the stability of the asset. Weights are inversely proportional to the asset's historical annualized standard deviation ($\sigma_i$).
	\begin{equation}
		w_i = \frac{1/\sigma_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} (1/\sigma_j)}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Advantages:} Penalizes high-volatility tech stocks and rewards stable dividend payers (e.g., KO, JNJ). This typically results in a smoother equity curve and a higher Sharpe Ratio.
		\item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Can underperform during strong bull markets driven by high-beta growth stocks; assumes historical volatility predicts future risk.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection{Geometric Rank Weighting (Paper Method)}
	Following the methodology proposed in \textit{Benchmarking Personalized Investing} (Schachter, 2025), we generated weights using a geometric progression parameter $r$ (set to $r=0.8$).
	
	\begin{quote}
		An algorithm that produces ranking is to have the $\{w_i\}$ be a monotonic decreasing function of the stock choices, i.e., $w_2 < w_1$, $w_3 < w_2$, ... For illustration is assumed the weights be based on a geometric series $\{s_i\}$
		$$s_i = ar^{i-1}$$
		Where $a>0$, $r<1$. The familiar result of the sum is
		$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i = a \frac{1-r^N}{1-r}$$
		Now, a convenient choice of normalization is 
		$$w_i = \frac{s_i}{\sum_i s_i}$$
		which simplifies to
		$$w_i = \frac{r^{i-1}(1-r)}{1-r^N}$$
		which is notably independent of $a$.
	\end{quote}
	
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Advantages:} Provides a parameterized way to test the ``skill'' of asset allocation. By generating a fixed set of weights and permuting their assignment across assets, we can statistically isolate the skill of the investor from the random luck of the weighting distribution.
		\item \textbf{Disadvantages:} The choice of parameter $r$ is subjective; higher $r$ values lead to aggressive concentration.
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Hedge Fund persona methodology}
		
	\subsection{Cross-Sectional Momentum Strategy}
	\label{sec:momentum}
	
	We implement a long/short equity momentum strategy that combines several well-documented enhancements shown to dramatically improve risk-adjusted performance.
	
	\subsubsection{Signal Construction}
	
	Short term reversal is strong on equities (see \cite{1}), hence the core signal that we use for this strategy is going to be the \textbf{12-1 month momentum}, which has been the most robust specification since \cite{1}. 
	
	We start by computing the cumulative return from day $t-252$ to $t-22$, i.e., 
	
	$$
	R_{i,t}^{(12-1)} = \prod_{k=22}^{252} (1 + r_{i,t-k}) - 1
	$$
	
	We use information from the last 252 trading years and skip the last trading month (days $t-1$ to $t-21$) to avoid the short-term reversal effect.
	
	It is shown in \cite{2} that conventional momentum strategies exhibit substantial time-varying exposures to the Fama and French factors.  Blitz, Huil and Martens also argued that these exposures can be reduced by ranking stocks on residual stock returns instead of total returns. As a consequence, residual momentum earns risk-adjusted profits that are about twice as large as those associated with total return momentum; is more consistent over time; and less concentrated in the extremes of the cross-section of stocks. 
	
	Hence, to isolate stock-specific momentum, we compute the \textbf{residual momentum}
	
	$$
	\tilde{r}_{i,t} = r_{i,t} - \hat{\beta}_{i,t} \cdot r_{m,t},
	\qquad
	\hat{\beta}_{i,t} = \frac{\text{Cov}_{252}(r_i, r_m)}{\text{Var}_{252}(r_m)}
	$$
	
	where $r_{m,t}$ is the market excess return (Fama-French Mkt-RF factor).
	
	\subsubsection{Sector-Neutral Ranking}
	
	To ensure that our momentum strategy is not accidentally just betting on sectors, but instead picking winners vs. losers within each sector, we standardize the signal within each GICS (Global Industry Classification Standard) sector, this way we actually isolate stock-specific momentum. 
	
	Otherwise, if an specific sector from our portfolio has a rally, all stocks within this sector will have high raw momentum, but this will correspond to a sector phenomenon, not stock alpha, hence, standardizing allow us to make signals comparable across different sectors,  given that the momentum strategy is supposed to capture company-specific performance persistence, not macroeconomic or industry cycles.
	
	We define our $z$-scores to be
	
	$$
	z_{i,t} = \frac{s_{i,t} - \mu_{\text{sector}(i),t}}{\sigma_{\text{sector}(i),t}}
	$$
	
	where $s_{i,t} \in \{ R_{i,t}^{(12-1)}, \tilde{r}_{i,t} \}$ depending on the specification.
	
	\subsubsection{Portfolio Construction with Volatility Scaling}
	\label{sec:vol-scaling}
	
	Another well-known issue in cross-sectional momentum portfolios is that a small number of high-volatility stocks tend to dominate the risk of the entire portfolio when equal weights are used. Because portfolio variance scales with the square of volatility, allocating equal dollar amounts to stocks with dramatically different risk levels produces highly imbalanced risk contributions. This distortion causes noisy performance, large drawdowns, and unstable factor exposures. To address this problem, we apply \textbf{inverse-volatility weighting}, which, I suspect, is the standard approach used in institutional equity factor portfolios to achieve risk parity within the long and short legs.
	
	Let $\hat{\sigma}_{i,t}$ denote the estimated ex-ante volatility of stock $i$ at time $t$, computed using a rolling 63-day (why? recent volatility) standard deviation of daily returns:
	\[
	\hat{\sigma}_{i,t} = 
	\sqrt{\frac{1}{62}\sum_{k=1}^{63} 
		\left( r_{i,t-k} - \bar{r}_{i,t} \right)^2 }.
	\]
	
	Given the cross-sectional signal $z_{i,t}$ defined before, that determines the rank ordering of stocks (e.g., sector-neutral residual momentum), the long and short portfolios are formed by selecting the top $N$ and bottom $N$ names, respectively. Within each leg, we scale the position of each stock by the inverse of its volatility
	\[
	w_{i,t}^{+} = \frac{\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{i,t}}}
	{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}_t} \frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{j,t}}},
	\qquad
	w_{i,t}^{-} = -
	\frac{\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{i,t}}}
	{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_t} \frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{j,t}}},
	\]
	where $\mathcal{L}_t$ and $\mathcal{S}_t$ denote the sets of long and short stocks at time $t$. These weights \textbf{guarantee} that every stock contributes approximately the same amount of risk to its respective portfolio leg. In contrast to equal weighting, where a  high-volatility stock can contribute more than five times the risk of a low-volatility stock, inverse-vol weighting stabilizes the contribution of each position and prevents extreme concentration.
	
	This construction effectively produces \textbf{risk-parity} long and short books. 
	Risk parity is desirable because academic and empirical evidence shows that momentum signals are substantially noisier in high-volatility stocks. These stocks tend to exhibit weaker signal persistence, stronger short-term reversals, and sharper crash risk during market rebounds. By shrinking their weights proportionally to their volatility, we reduce the impact of these unstable names on both tail behavior and day-to-day fluctuations in the portfolio's P\&L.
	
	\subsubsection{Rebalancing Frequency}
	
	Momentum signals decay rapidly over time, and a strategy that only updates positions monthly may fail to capture a large fraction of the available alpha. To address this, we rebalance the portfolio on a weekly basis, i.e., every five trading days. Weekly rebalancing allows the strategy to more accurately follow the evolving cross-sectional signal, reducing lag and improving the persistence of factor returns. While daily rebalancing is theoretically even more responsive, it may lead to excessive transaction costs. Empirical evidence \cite{3} shows that weekly rebalancing significantly enhances Sharpe ratios and improves the efficiency of momentum capture compared to monthly rebalancing.
	
	\subsubsection{Volatility Target}
	
	Even after inverse-volatility weighting, the overall portfolio risk fluctuates over time due to changes in market volatility. 
	To stabilize risk and make returns comparable across different periods, we scale daily portfolio returns to target a constant annualized volatility $\sigma_{\text{target}}$:
	\[
	r_{p,t}^{\text{target}} = r_{p,t} \times \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{p,t}},
	\]
	where $\hat{\sigma}_{p,t}$ is the ex-ante estimate of the portfolio's realized volatility. 
	
	Volatility targeting has several benefits, it removes bias due to volatility timing, produces a smoother equity curve, reduces drawdowns, and makes Sharpe ratios more stable and meaningful. 
	Studies in \cite{barroso} demonstrate that combining momentum with volatility targeting materially improves risk-adjusted performance.
	
	The strategy return prior to volatility targeting is simply:
	\[
	r_{p,t} = \sum_{i} w_{i,t-1}\, r_{i,t}.
	\]
	
	To ensure constant risk-taking, we scale the strategy's daily returns so that its \textit{ex-ante} annualized volatility equals a fixed target $\sigma^* = 10\%$.
	
	Let $\hat{\sigma}_{p,t}^{\text{daily}}$ be the 21-trading-day rolling standard deviation of raw strategy returns up to day $t-1$. The annualized volatility is then estimated as
	$$
	\hat{\sigma}_{p,t} = \hat{\sigma}_{p,t}^{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{252}.
	$$
	
	The required leverage factor is
	$$
	\lambda_t = \frac{\sigma^*}{\hat{\sigma}_{p,t}}
	= \frac{0.10}{\hat{\sigma}_{p,t}}.
	$$
	
	To prevent extreme leverage during volatility spikes or ultra-low volatility regimes, we apply bounds:
	$$
	\tilde{\lambda}_t 
	= \min\!\Big(4,\; \max\!\big(0.2,\; \lambda_t\big)\Big).
	$$
	
	In practice, you want to avoid:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Leverage $> 4\times$ $\to$ blow-up risk in volatility spikes (e.g. March 2020).
		\item Leverage $< 0.2\times$ $\to$ strategy becomes inactive during ultra-low vol regimes.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The volatility-targeted return is therefore
	$$
	r_{p,t}^{\text{target}} = \tilde{\lambda}_t \times r_{p,t}.
	$$
	
	This procedure, introduced on \cite{barroso} and refined on \cite{moreira}, dramatically improves the Sharpe ratio of momentum and other trend-following strategies by mitigating the ``volatility drag'' and crash risk associated with unscaled exposure.
	
	\subsubsection{Performance Metrics}
	
	Performance is evaluated using standard metrics:
	
	\begin{align}
		\text{Annualized Return} &\quad &= \left( \prod (1 + r_{p,t}^{\text{target}}) \right)^{252/T} - 1 \\
		\text{Sharpe Ratio} &\quad &= \frac{\mathbb{E}[r_p^{\text{target}} - r_f] \cdot 252}{\sigma(r_p^{\text{target}}) \cdot \sqrt{252}} \\
		\text{Maximum Drawdown} &\quad &= \min_t \left( \frac{W_t}{ \max_{0 \leq s \leq t} W_s } - 1 \right)
	\end{align}
	
	where $W_t = \prod_{k=1}^t (1 + r_{p,k}^{\text{target}})$ is the wealth index.

	On our portfolio constituents (2015--2025), the strategy delivers:
	
	\begin{center}

	These results confirm that combining residual momentum, sector neutralization, volatility scaling, and frequent rebalancing, and volatility targeting produces a superior risk-adjusted profile compared to naive momentum implementations.		
		
	
	\section{Index Tracking Implementation}
	Finally, we try to replicate our portfolio returns, considering the most reasonable weighting strategy, i.e., the geometrically weighted one with $r=0.8$. We consider that our portfolio returns is an index that we want to replicate by buying other stocks within the S\&P500 universe. 
	
	We define our optimization problem for finding a new tracking portfolio as
	
	$$
	\min_{\vec{x}} \epsilon_{MAX}(\vec{x})
	$$
	subject to:
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(x_i) \leq K
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N \beta_{\sigma}^{+}(x_i) \leq 0
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N x_i + \sum_{i=1}^N a_i |x_i - x_i^0| + A\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(|x_i - x_i^0|) \leq C
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N a_i |x_i - x_i^0| + A\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(|x_i - x_i^0|) \leq \gamma C
	$$
	$$
	x_i \geq 0, \quad i=1, \ldots, I
	$$
	
	by considering a mixed-integer linear programming (MIP) formulation of the previous equations, we have that this is equivalent to 
	$$
	\min_{y,\vec{x},\vec{v},\vec{w},\vec{z}} y
	$$
	subject to:
	$$
	y \geq \sum_{i=1}^N \theta_{ti}x_i - \theta_{t0}, \quad t=1,\ldots,T
	$$
	$$
	y \geq -\left(\sum_{i=1}^N \theta_{ti}x_i - \theta_{t0}\right), \quad t=1,\ldots,T
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N v_i \leq K
	$$
	$$
	l_i v_i \leq x_i \leq u_i v_i, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	z_i \geq x_i - x_i^0, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	z_i \geq -(x_i - x_i^0), \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	z_i \leq Uw_i, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N x_i + \sum_{i=1}^N a_i z_i + A\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \leq C
	$$
	$$
	\sum_{i=1}^N a_i z_i + A\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \leq \gamma C
	$$
	$$
	v_i \in \{0,1\}, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	w_i \in \{0,1\}, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	$$
	x_i \geq 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,N
	$$
	
	Now we proceed to input the data to the PSG solver and run the optimization in R, when doing this we obtain the following tracking portfolio. 
	
	We use the following constraints and parameters:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Timeframe of analysis: 2020-09-15 to 2025-09-11
		\item Cardinality constraints: $K=15$
		\item Normalization $\gamma = 0.015$
		\item Buy-in constraint (min position allowed) $=\text{\$15,000}$
		\item new tracking portfolio value $+$ transaction cost $\leq$ \$1,000,000 
		\item We assume the transaction costs are 1.5\% the maximum value of the portfolio
		\item Box constraints: We don't allow shorts and one position can't exceed 40\% of the maximum budget for the portfolio. ``Box: $\geq$ 0, $\leq$ 400,000''
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Conclusion}
	
	The results of the project demonstrate that portfolio performance is highly sensitive to the weighting methodology and the investor persona considered. The Buy \& Hold investor experienced smoother long-horizon returns, where structural choices—such as avoiding concentration risk—played a more influential role than short-term volatility. Conversely, the Hedge Fund persona, with daily P\&L sensitivity, exhibited a strong dependence on volatility regimes, fat-tailed shocks, and short-term deviations in market behavior. This highlights that strategies yielding strong long-term compounding may be far less attractive when assessed under a high-frequency risk lens.
	
	The analysis of weighting methods revealed that naïve equal weighting provides broad diversification but underperforms during periods dominated by mega-cap growth stocks. Traditional market-capitalization weighting achieved higher absolute and risk-adjusted returns but suffered from extreme concentration in Apple and Microsoft, making the portfolio highly sensitive to idiosyncratic shocks of a few constituents. The geometrically weighting approach offered a balanced compromise, preserving size hierarchy while preventing excessive overweighting of the largest firms.
	
	Finally, the index-tracking optimization produced portfolios capable of closely imitating the target equity portfolio under realistic constraints such as cardinality and long-only weights. Although perfect replication is unattainable without allowing short positions or a large number of constituents, the tracking portfolios achieved relatively low tracking error and stable performance.
